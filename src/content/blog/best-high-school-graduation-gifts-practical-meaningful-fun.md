Congratulations to the grad in your life! High school graduation is a milestone that opens many paths—college, trade school, a gap year, or a first job. ## How to choose a high school graduation gift

A great graduation gift sits at the intersection of usefulness and personal meaning. Start here:

- Consider their next step: Are they heading to college, trade school, the military, a gap year, or a job? Tailor the gift to their immediate needs.
- Go practical, then add personality: Combine an everyday essential with a small keepsake or hand-written note.
- Think portability and durability: If they’re moving into a dorm or apartment, compact and sturdy wins.
- Plan your budget: Spend what’s comfortable. Etiquette experts like the Emily Post Institute note that thoughtful gifts or heartfelt cards are always appropriate—there’s no fixed amount you must spend (see source below).
- Presentation matters: Even cash and gift cards can be memorable with a creative delivery.

Sources for context and etiquette:

- Emily Post Institute on graduation gift-giving etiquette: https://emilypost.com/advice/graduation-gift-giving-etiquette
- National Retail Federation (NRF) consumer research consistently shows cash and gift cards are top graduation gifts: https://nrf.com
- FTC tips to avoid gift card scams (good to know if you’re gifting a card): https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/gift-card-scams

## Creative ways to give cash or gift cards

Cash and gift cards can feel personal when you package them with intention.

- “Open when…” envelopes: Label with moments—“First day on campus,” “Homesick night,” “Big exam week.” Tuck a small bill or gift card and a note into each.
- Scholarship from you: Write a card that says, “This is a $100 ‘Mini‑Emergency Scholarship’ for textbooks or groceries.”
- Treasure hunt: Hide clues around the house leading to the card. Film the fun for a memory you’ll both keep.
- Pair it with a tiny tool: Tape a coffee shop card to a reusable mug, or a bookstore card to a bookmark.

## What to spend on a graduation gift?

There’s no universal right amount. Your relationship, local norms, and budget should guide you. Some families choose a larger gift for immediate family members and smaller tokens for family friends or classmates. The Emily Post Institute emphasizes that a heartfelt note is always appropriate, even without a gift: https://emilypost.com/advice/graduation-gift-giving-etiquette

If you pool funds with relatives, consider a single “anchor gift” (luggage, laptop backpack, or headphones) plus a group card everyone signs.

## A week‑of‑move‑in checklist to turn your gift into action

Help your grad set up quickly with this practical “first week” checklist (add it to your card!):

- Back up your laptop to your new portable SSD before leaving home.
- Label your power strip, chargers, and headphones with your name or initials.
- Scan key documents (ID, medical cards, car info) and save copies in a secure cloud folder.
- Pack a “first night” tote: sheets, pillowcase, small tool kit, power strip, and an extra phone charger.
- Make a money plan for your gift card or cash—three line items to start: textbooks, food, and transit.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- Over‑sized items: Dorms are small. When in doubt, choose compact versions.
- Duplicate appliances: Some residence halls restrict certain appliances—check the housing list before purchasing.
- Unverified gift card sellers: Buy gift cards directly from reputable retailers (FTC guidance: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/gift-card-scams).
